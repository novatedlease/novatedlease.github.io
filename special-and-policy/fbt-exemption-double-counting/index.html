Don’t double count the hospital / NFP FBT cap when judging a novated lease

By changyang1230 · Updated 5 November 2026

A very common claim in hospital and NFP circles goes something like:

“If you get FBT exemption from your workplace, novated lease is worth it. Most health places or NFPs offer it.”

Sometimes that’s true.

But it’s also very easy to double count the hospital / NFP salary packaging cap and mistakenly attribute an existing benefit as “extra saving from the car”, when in reality you have simply redirected the same capped benefit away from living expenses and towards the novated lease.

This page explains the logic, and how to think about it properly.

The cap is real — and it’s limited

The usual FBT exemption for eligible employers is capped:

  • $9,010 for public hospitals (and some rebatable employers), and
  • $15,900 for a public benevolent institution (PBI) or health promotion charity.

Most of the time, employees can already salary-package:

  • everyday living expenses, and/or
  • rent / mortgage-related payments (via approved mechanisms), and/or
  • credit card repayments that are linked to living expenses,

up to that cap.

The cap is finite.

The key question: would you have used the cap anyway?

The first question I always ask is:

Without this novated lease, would you otherwise have been able to use the $9,010 / $15,900 cap on living expenses (up to the cap)?

If the answer is yes, then packaging a petrol-car novated lease under that cap is usually not an additional perk.

You have simply used the same pot of FBT-exempt benefit for the car instead of living expenses.

This is why it is inaccurate to treat the $9,010 / $15,900 cap itself as “net saving from the novated lease” if you were already able to use it anyway.

Most people can use it up just with rent / mortgage / credit card repayments.

A common scenario: “I paused my home-loan packaging to package the car”

People often describe doing something like:

I’ve ceased/paused my home-loan based FBT exemption packaging to package the car instead.”

This is a very understandable approach — but it highlights the point.

If you were previously using the cap on home-loan / living expenses, and you stop doing that to package the car instead, then the cap itself is not an extra benefit created by the novated lease.

It’s the same capped benefit, just redirected.

How to evaluate a petrol-car novated lease in this setting (without double counting)

If you are already maxing out the $9,010 / $15,900 cap using living expenses, then to assess whether a petrol-car novated lease is worth it, you should mentally frame the comparison as:

Evaluate the car lease as if you didn’t have additional cap available, because you don’t.

In other words:

  • The cap is already “spent” in the counterfactual baseline (living expenses).
  • Packaging the car under the cap is mainly a swap of what you salary package, not a new source of savings.

If you were already able to spend the cap on living expenses etc, then make sure that your novated lease provider generates a quote without assuming the use of this FBT-exemption cap.

I have once seen an ICE novated lease quote which has applied the $9,010 FBT-exemption cap automatically in their quote, producing a claim of saving that is invalid once the cap has already been used elsewhere.

Always double check what assumption is being made in your quote.

What if the cap is used on the car instead? Cap vs ECM

There are two ways a hospital or NFP employee can handle the FBT on a petrol-car novated lease:

  • Employee contribution method (ECM). You pay the FBT-equivalent amount from post-tax salary, the taxable value falls to zero, and your living-expense packaging carries on as before. This is the structure most packaging companies describe when they say a novated lease sits “above the cap”. Maxxia, for example, says a novated lease “can be taken out in addition to any existing salary packaging caps you have”.
  • Use the cap on the car. The car’s grossed-up taxable value is counted against your FBT exemption cap, so no ECM is needed. The cap is then no longer available for living expenses. This option is not offered by every employer; Smartsalary’s Queensland booklet describes it as “a third alternative… for certain employees of Queensland Health hospitals, Queensland Ambulance Service, and Legal Aid Queensland”.

So “above the cap” is accurate for the ECM route, but not if your provider uses the cap on the car.

The only extra saving is the ECM GST

The post-tax ECM contribution is treated as payment for a taxable supply, so one eleventh of it is paid as GST, and that GST is taken from your pre-tax salary. Using the cap on the car means there is no ECM, and so no ECM GST. That is the entire difference between the two routes.

In a typical example (public hospital employee on $100,000, ICE car with a $45,050 base value, 5-year lease, already packaging the full $9,010), the after-tax cost of the car is about $13,554 a year with the cap on the car and about $14,111 a year with ECM. The gap is roughly $557 a year (about $21 a fortnight), which is the after-tax cost of the ECM GST ($819 at 32% marginal tax). Slightly higher reportable income on the cap route can claw a little of that back through HELP repayments or the childcare subsidy, so treat $557 as an upper limit.

Note that neither route is “free”. You still pay $13,500 to $14,100 a year after tax to run the car, and the cap is spent either way.

Show the full working

I used this site’s calculator (see section 1.1, the Summary) and these assumptions:

  • Public hospital employee, $100,000 salary (30% + 2% Medicare), already packaging the full $9,010 living-expense cap.
  • ICE car with a base value of $45,050, a 5-year lease and a 10% effective interest rate. The calculator gives a vehicle lease of $9,612.83 a year and I assumed running costs of $6,080 a year.
  • Take-home figures below use 2025-26 tax rates. The calculator link starts the lease in December 2026, so it uses the 2026-27 rates (15% first bracket) and shows take-home pay about $268 higher in each column. The gaps between columns are the same.

The car’s statutory taxable value is $45,050 × 20% = $9,010, which grosses up to $9,010 × 1.8868 = $17,000. That is exactly the public hospital cap, so the car happens to fit.

Scenario A: no car, living expenses packaged (the starting point)

  • Pre-tax deduction: $9,010 of living expenses.
  • Taxable income: $100,000 − $9,010 = $90,990.
  • Tax saving from packaging: $9,010 × 32% = $2,883.20.
  • Income tax and Medicare on $90,990 come to about $19,905, so take-home pay is about $71,085.
  • RFBA: $9,010 × 1.8868 = $17,000.

Scenario B: the cap is used on the car, living-expense packaging stops

  • Pre-tax deduction: the whole lease and running cost, $9,612.83 + $6,080 = $15,692.83. The cap covers the FBT, so there is no ECM and no ECM GST.
  • Taxable income: $100,000 − $15,692.83 = $84,307.17.
  • Income tax and Medicare come to about $17,766, so take-home pay is about $66,541.
  • You now pay living expenses from take-home pay: $66,541 − $9,010 = $57,531.
  • RFBA: $17,000, now from the car.

Scenario C: ECM on the car, living-expense packaging kept

  • The calculator’s pre-tax deduction is $9,612.83 + $6,080 − $9,010 (ECM) + $819.09 (ECM GST on $9,010 ÷ 11) = $7,501.92. The $9,010 ECM comes out of take-home pay.
  • The calculator shows that $7,501.92 as a $5,101.30 after-tax cost ($7,501.92 × 0.68). That is the take-home impact, not the pre-tax deduction. Don’t use it to work out taxable income.
  • Taxable income: $100,000 − $9,010 (living) − $7,501.92 = $83,488.08.
  • Income tax and Medicare come to about $17,504, so take-home pay is about $65,984.
  • Less the post-tax ECM: $65,984 − $9,010 = $56,974.
  • RFBA: the ECM brings the car’s RFBA to nil, but the living-expense packaging still gives $17,000, so the total is $17,000.

Why B is ahead of C

  • C has $819.09 more pre-tax deductions than B ($9,010 + $7,501.92 = $16,511.92 against $15,692.83). That extra $819.09 is the ECM GST: a real cost that you merely get 32% tax relief on.
  • Net cost of the ECM GST: $819.09 × (1 − 0.32) = $556.98 a year, or roughly $21 per fortnight.
  • That matches the gap in the after-tax figures: $57,531 − $56,974 ≈ $557.

The $557 is real, but it is not the $9,010 cap “paying for the car”. In both routes you still pay about $13,500 to $14,100 a year after tax to run the car, and the cap is spent either way.

HECS and childcare subsidy

RFBA is $17,000 in all three columns, so the reportable amounts barely move between B and C. HECS repayment income (taxable income plus the full RFBA) is $107,990 in A, $101,307 in B and $100,488 in C. For childcare subsidy, hospital and PBI employees count only 53% of RFBA, which is $9,010, so the adjusted taxable income is $100,000 in A, $93,317 in B and $92,498 in C. The difference between B and C is just the $819.09 less pre-tax deduction in B.

Limits of the cap route

  • The car has to fit under the cap. A base value much above $45,050 uses more than $17,000 grossed up, and the excess is taxed (or needs ECM). The cap is per employee per FBT year (April to March), and the Driversnote guide notes that a salary-packaged or novated-lease car counts against it. PBIs and health promotion charities have a larger cap ($15,900, or $30,000 grossed up), so a car fits more easily.
  • Your employer or provider must allow it. Many offer ECM only.

EVs are a separate topic

It’s worth separating two different concepts:

  • The hospital / NFP cap (which is limited and easy to fill with living expenses), versus
  • The EV FBT exemption, which can apply even for non-exempt employers (as long as the EV is eligible).

So:

  • If you are talking about petrol cars under the hospital/NFP cap, double counting is the big trap.
  • If you are talking about eligible EVs, you are not at risk of double counting.

The takeaway

If you remember nothing else:

If you were already able to use the $9,010 / $15,900 cap for living expenses, then using it for an ICE novated lease is usually not “extra saving” — it’s mainly a reallocation of the same capped benefit.

That said, if your employer lets you use the cap on the car, doing so is usually slightly better than ECM, because it avoids the ECM GST (about $557 a year in the example above). It is a small extra, not a reason to count the cap itself as a saving.

Make sure that any quote generated uses the correct assumption if you are a hospital / NFP employee, have already used the $9,010, and are looking at an ICE NL.

Dr Michael Keane, a Melbourne anaesthetist, is taking a Victorian health service to the Victorian Supreme Court to obtain the unredacted contract between the hospital and its exclusive salary packaging provider.
