# You don't have to sell your car above residual value

There is a common myth in novated lease discussion that goes:

> Novated lease is worth it when / if you could sell your car for more than the residual value you pay out. 

This rule of thumb has gotten such a big following such that many people use that as the sole rule behind whether their deal is worthwhile. They look at their quote, find out what their 1/2/3/4/5 year residual is, work out how much their expected sales price is, and take that as the shortcut of whether they are "making profit" out of novated lease. 

The fact is – this way of evaluating novated lease is simply invalid. 

If you look at the [novated lease calculator](../calculator/index.md), you would see that it's structured as the following: 

- cashflow: how much it costs you out of pocket over the lease period AND the residual value paid at the end to own the car outright. 
- liability: how much does the financing method affect the home loan interest 
- asset: how much is the asset (i.e. the car) worth at the end of the lease. 

When two financing methods are compared (or even new-car-via-NL vs keeping old car), the all three categories are juxtaposed between the financing methods, and the overall discrepancy of the three categories are taken as the "global financial position" difference i.e. how much is the net worth different by. 

When you break it down this way, the "just compare the residual value and the sales price" immediately breaks down. 

The residual value is determined from the beginning of any lease based on the financed amount and the lease duration, based on [ATO's mandatory minimum residual formula](../costs-and-savings/why-residual-values-matter.md). Therefore it is a constant once you start a lease. 

Meanwhile, the sales price goes into the "asset" in my breakdown. 

Now observe this: 

- whether or not you bought the car via cash or novated lease, you will be able to sell the car for the same price when you get rid of it at the end of the lease (if you choose not to keep it). Therefore, comparing a car-via-NL vs a car-via-cash would always have the same "asset" value – which is also why the asset value is not presented in such comparison in the summary view of the calculator output as they always cancel out. 
- if you do compare new-car-via-NL vs keeping-current-car then the end-asset value would indeed be different and would indeed go into the overall calculation. Note however that this comparison is only dependent on the relative difference between the new car's estimated market value at the end-of-lease, vs the original car's estimated market value at the same timepoint. Again, there is zero relationship between this particular figure's relative relationship with the ATO mandatory residual value. 

## The psychological factor

A lot of people have anchored the "worthiness" of novated lease by examining the relationship between the residual value vs market value, probably for the following reasons:

> If the market value is lower than the residual value, and if the payout and the sales of the car take place close to one another, then there is a short term acute shortfall in cash. In other words, if you paid out $23,235 to own the car, and then sell the car at only $21,000, then you would have an acute $2,235 shortfall in your wallet in that timeframe. 

However, what they fail to consider is that the overall saving is based on how much they have paid and how much liability differences they have made over the lifetime of the lease, not merely the cashflow in the two months of your paying out the lease and selling it. 


## Example using my own lease

However, what people fail to appreciate is that this is only one minor part of the overall calculation of the novated lease's cost differential compared to other financing methods. For example, using my own lease (which is the default figures in the calculator):

- the 5-year cost of novated lease is $46,608 less than if I bought the car outright. 
- The calculator had used a ~40% original price as the guesstimate of the car's market value in 5 years - which is $33,000, way above the residual payout of $23,235. 
- If you manually change the residual value to $21,000 - guess what happens to the overall 5-year saving: NOTHING. 
- As explained in the earlier example, the market value at 5-year mark cancels out between novated lease pathway and cash pathway, therefore the saving of NL vs cash would have zero effect however much the car is sold for. And the car's value by definition is not affected by whether it was initially funded with cash or novated lease. 


# The key takeaway
> It is fallacious to use the relative difference between market value at end-of-lease and the residual value to evaluate a novated lease's savings.

# You Don’t Have to Sell Your Car Above the Residual Value

There is a common myth in novated lease discussions:

> “A novated lease is only worth it if you can sell the car for more than the residual value.”

This rule of thumb has become so widespread that many people use it as the primary — or even sole — test of whether their lease is worthwhile. They check their 1/2/3/4/5‑year residual, estimate the expected market value at the end, and treat the difference as a shortcut for determining whether they are “making a profit.”

This way of evaluating a novated lease is fundamentally flawed.

---

## How the Calculator Actually Evaluates a Lease

If you look at the [novated lease calculator](../calculator/index.md), it separates the analysis into three components:

- **Cashflow** – How much you pay out of pocket over the lease period, including the residual paid at the end if you choose to own the car.
- **Liability** – How the financing method affects home loan interest (or opportunity cost of capital).
- **Asset** – The value of the car at the end of the lease.

When comparing two financing methods (e.g. novated lease vs cash purchase, or new car via NL vs keeping your current car), all three categories are compared side‑by‑side. The difference across these categories determines the overall change in your financial position — effectively, the net worth difference.

Once you view it this way, the “just compare residual to market value” shortcut immediately breaks down.

---

## Why the Residual vs Market Value Comparison Fails

The residual value is determined at the start of the lease. It is based on:

- The financed amount
- The lease duration
- The [ATO’s mandatory minimum residual formula](../costs-and-savings/why-residual-values-matter.md)

Once the lease begins, the residual is fixed.

The market value at the end of the lease, on the other hand, is simply the value of the car as an asset.

Now consider the key point:

- Whether you bought the car with cash or via novated lease, you can sell it for the same market price at the end. The funding method does not change the car’s resale value.

When comparing **novated lease vs cash purchase of the same car**, the end asset value is identical in both scenarios. It therefore cancels out in the comparison — which is why the asset value does not appear in the summary comparison view of the calculator.

If you are comparing **new car via NL vs keeping your current car**, then yes — the end asset values differ. But that difference depends only on the relative market values of the two cars at the same future time point.

It has nothing to do with how that market value compares to the ATO residual.

---

## The Psychological Anchor

Why does this myth persist?

Because of short‑term cash psychology.

Consider this situation:

> You pay out a $23,235 residual to own the car. You immediately sell it for $21,000. You experience a $2,235 shortfall in that moment.

That shortfall feels real and painful.

However, this focuses only on the cash movement at the end of the lease. It ignores:

- How much you paid throughout the lease
- How much tax you saved
- How your liabilities changed over time
- The total financial position across the entire lease duration

Novated lease savings (or losses) are determined over the full lifecycle of the arrangement — not in the two months surrounding payout and sale.

---

## Example Using My Own Lease

Using my own lease (the default figures in the calculator):

- Over 5 years, the novated lease costs **$46,608 less** than purchasing the car outright.
- The calculator assumes a 5‑year market value of roughly 40% of the original price (~$33,000), which is well above the $23,235 residual.

Now here is the critical test:

If you manually change the assumed market value to $21,000 instead of $33,000, the overall 5‑year saving compared to cash remains unchanged.

Why?

Because when comparing novated lease vs cash purchase of the same car, the asset value at year 5 cancels out in both pathways. The resale value does not depend on how the car was funded.

Therefore, the relative relationship between market value and residual value does not determine whether the novated lease “made money.”

---

# The Key Takeaway

> It is incorrect to evaluate a novated lease purely by comparing the end‑of‑lease market value with the residual payout.

The residual is a financing construct determined at the beginning of the lease.

The market value is simply the asset value of the car.

They operate in different parts of the calculation — and conflating them leads to misleading conclusions.
