# When your employer has an exclusive novated lease provider

A novated lease is supposed to be a competitive product. For a lot of Australian employees it isn't — because the employer has signed an **exclusive contract with a single provider**, making that one company the only legal path to a novated lease through their payroll.

The provider becomes the gatekeeper between the employee and the tax incentive. The employer has no commercial reason to question the arrangement, because the cost falls on the employee rather than the employer's books. The provider, in turn, has no competitive pressure on price.

## The three structures, and why one of them is the problem

Most large Australian employers don't run salary packaging in-house. They contract it out. The question is *how* they contract it out:

- **Panel of providers (acceptable)** — the employer approves more than one providers; the employee picks one based on quote. Some competitive pressure on price.
- **BYO / self-managed permitted (best)** — the employer allows the employee to bring any underlying financier, or in some rare cases fully self-manage the lease. Full competition.
- **Single exclusive provider (the issue)** — one contracted provider, no panel, no BYO allowed. The employee has no negotiating power.

In the third structure the provider knows exactly one thing: **the employee has nowhere else to go.** They apply this knowledge to maximise profit. 

The traditional setup at very large employers e.g. hospitals, state government, major organisations is exactly this third structure.

## How to spot you're locked in

The clearest single signal is **the effective interest rate on your lease quote**.

Competitive open-market rates for a standard 5-year EV novated lease sit at around **7–9%**. If your quote comes back in the **low-to-mid teens** — and especially if it's in the 13–17% range — that is highly suggestive of an exclusive-provider arrangement extracting margin that competitive pricing wouldn't sustain. The rate is the symptom; the structure is the cause.

Cross-check your employer in the community-maintained **[Employer BYO Checker](../tools/byo-employer-check/)** — it catalogues what is publicly known about specific Australian employers' arrangements, including which are exclusive and which permit BYO / self-managed leases.

## Why the structure exists — the rent-seeking dynamic

The mechanism is straightforward, and it doesn't depend on anyone behaving improperly:

- The employer signs an exclusive contract with one provider.
- The provider charges the **employees** (via inflated effective interest rates, fees, and undisclosed margins on running costs), not the employer.
- The employer treats salary packaging as a **no-cost employee benefit** — admin is outsourced, payroll is simplified, and the financial cost falls on the employee, not the organisation.
- Whatever the provider pays back to the employer — sign-on fees, per-employee payments, rebates, volume bonuses — is funded by the employees on those leases.

Whether you call that a kickback, a service fee, or a routine commercial arrangement is partly a matter of perspective. What is harder to argue is the consequence: **the entity choosing the provider is not the entity paying for the choice.** That is a classic principal-agent problem — and in markets where it goes unchecked, providers reliably extract margins that competition would otherwise discipline.

This sits inside the broader pattern of [why novated leasing is poorly regulated](/special-and-policy/why-poorly-regulated/) in Australia — no product disclosure statement, no standardised comparison rate, minimal disclosure of provider commissions. The exclusive-provider arrangement is one of its most concrete consequences.

### A documented case: Monash Health and Maxxia

In **March 2024**, *news.com.au* journalist Michelle Bowes [^1] reported on the salary packaging arrangement between **Monash Health** (Victoria's largest public health service, 22,000 staff) and **Maxxia** (Australia's largest salary packaging provider). The reporting alleged that:

- Monash Health received an **annual sign-on fee of $3–5 million** from Maxxia under their exclusive novated leasing contract.
- A commercial lawyer's analysis put Maxxia's effective interest rate to staff at **15–17%**, against a wider market rate of around **8%**.
- When Monash Health awarded the contract in 2017, only two providers were considered, and the contract was allegedly awarded based on which provider offered the highest sign-on fee.
- Staff — including Monash anaesthetist Dr Michael Keane and both the Senior and Junior Medical Staff Associations — had raised concerns about the high rates throughout 2023.
- A right to self-managed leasing was reportedly extended to Monash Health staff from November 2023 in response to those concerns, but per the article had not been communicated to staff at time of publication.
- Separately, the article alleged that Monash Health was retaining fuel tax credits (the GST on staff fuel spend) that should normally be returned to the employee under a typical novated lease — see [failure to pass on GST savings](/running-costs/failure-to-pass-gst-saving/) for the general mechanic.

Dr Keane lodged a Freedom of Information request for the underlying contract. After significant delay and pushback, the redacted Maxxia–Monash Health salary packaging contract was eventually released — you can read it [here](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/wvd10qw14lg70pd14a3jg/Maxxia-Salary-Packaging_Redacted-Highlighted.pdf?rlkey=c3iavk7yp5q2dov0dt9fjpaex&dl=0).

In practice, the release is **almost entirely useless for verifying the underlying allegations**: every schedule containing the financial terms — sign-on fees, payments, commission structures, commercial provisions — has been fully blacked out. What the released document does establish is that an exclusive contract of this form exists, and that the financial schedules are precisely the part the parties have fought hardest to keep from public view. That is itself a meaningful data point about the arrangement's transparency, and the reason Dr Keane's transparency campaign is ongoing.

Maxxia did not comment on specific contractual arrangements. Monash Health, in its formal response to *news.com.au*, said staff have a choice of financier from a Maxxia-managed panel or via a self-managed lease.

These remain **allegations as reported**. But the rate spread named in the reporting — *~8% market vs 15–17% locked-in* — is consistent with what employees at similar large-employer arrangements have been reporting for years, and it is the figure used throughout the rest of this page.

## What it actually costs — the margin framework

The underlying wholesale finance rate for a novated lease is around **7–8%**. Everything above that is provider margin — split between the leasing company, the financier, and (where applicable) the kickback to the employer. From observed quotes across a wide range of providers:

- **Competitive / BYO providers**: effective rates around **7–9%**. The provider extracts a modest margin of **$3,000–$5,000 from the tax incentive over a 5-year lease**. The provider still makes money; you still get most of the tax benefit.
- **Locked-in exclusive arrangements**: effective rates frequently in the **13–17% range**. The provider extracts **$10,000–$20,000 over a 5-year lease**, sometimes more.

That gap — between fair and predatory margin — is what the exclusive arrangement costs you. It is never a line item; it is buried in the repayments and amortised over the term. You can model it specifically in the [novated lease calculator](/calculator/) by adjusting the effective interest rate input.

:::note[It's the structure, not your credit profile]
If you ever ask why your effective rate is 14% when car loans are 7%, you will sometimes be told it reflects "novated lease risk" or "your credit profile". **It almost never does.** A novated lease is salary-deducted and arguably *less* risky to the financier than an arms-length car loan. A 14% rate has nothing to do with your risk profile — it reflects what the provider can charge given the absence of competition.
:::


:::tip[A trend worth knowing — advocacy works]
A year or two ago, exclusive-provider effective rates at the largest employers were routinely **14–16%**. They have come down to roughly **10–12%** at many of them — not because the industry has had a change of heart, but because increasing public awareness, FOI campaigns, employee organising, and media coverage have made the higher rates harder to defend. The Monash/Maxxia case is one of the more visible drivers of that change. The lesson: visibility shifts behaviour.
:::


### The honest caveat — and a less-discussed risk

It is important not to overstate the case.

Even at a predatory 15% effective rate, many employees still come out ahead of paying cash or taking a conventional car loan — the tax incentive is large enough to absorb a lot of margin before disappearing. That is partly *why* these arrangements persist: staff sign up, do save money, and never realise they could have saved much more.

But a high effective interest rate has a less-discussed second-order effect: it pushes the lease's **break-even point** much further out, leaving you exposed if it ends early.

A novated lease has substantial sunk costs in its early years (interest-heavy repayments plus an unamortised residual). At a competitive ~8% effective rate, cumulative net benefit overtakes those sunk costs quickly — break-even typically arrives inside year 1–2. At a locked-in 15% rate, break-even can be pushed well into the second half of the lease term, meaning an early termination event (redundancy, resignation, total loss) in years 1–3 can wipe out the entire benefit and then some. You can see this directly in **Section 7 of the [novated lease calculator](/calculator/)**, and the resulting magnitudes in the [early termination article](/risks/how-bad-can-early-termination-get/).

So three things are true at once:

1. The exclusive provider is extracting a much larger margin than competition would allow.
2. The employee, even bearing that margin, is often still better off than no lease — *in the absence of early termination*.
3. The employee is also carrying meaningfully more early-termination risk every year the lease runs, because break-even is pushed deep into the term.

The argument isn't *"don't get a novated lease"*. It is: **if your provider's margin is being inflated by an exclusive arrangement, you are leaving thousands to tens of thousands of dollars on the table that you didn't need to — and accepting years of additional early-termination exposure to do it.**

## What you can actually do

### 1. Get the policy in writing — and ask HR the right question

Get the written salary packaging policy. Look for the words *panel*, *BYO*, *self-managed*, *external provider*. If they're absent, you may be locked in. If they're present, you have leverage.

If you go to HR, the wording matters. Don't ask *"do you offer novated leasing?"* — they'll say yes and route you back to the locked-in provider. Ask:

> *"Does the employer permit BYO or self-managed novated leasing arrangements through a provider of my choice? If not, what is the basis for restricting it?"*

That phrasing forces an explicit, ideally documented answer.

### 2. Don't ask the locked-in provider whether BYO is allowed — they have a conflict of interest

This is the single most useful piece of advice on this page.

The provider's business model depends on the answer being "no". They will commonly **deflect, downplay, or simply tell you it isn't available**, even when it is — and even when it's actually written into their contract with the employer.

Instead, ask **independent BYO specialists** — they have no commercial reason to suppress the answer, and will typically check eligibility (and quote you) at no cost. The [Employer BYO Checker](../tools/byo-employer-check/) lists currently-active BYO operators alongside what's publicly known about specific employers. The larger Australian personal finance communities (Reddit's [r/NovatedLeasingAU](https://www.reddit.com/r/NovatedLeasingAU/), the major novated lease Facebook groups) will also surface current operators when asked.

### 3. Escalate via your union or professional association

If you work in healthcare, education, or government, your union or professional body has structural leverage you don't have as an individual. Several have already successfully pushed employers to broaden their provider panel or allow BYO — Dr Keane's Monash Health campaign is the highest-profile example. Awareness at the collective level often accelerates change at the employer level.

### 4. Decide whether the lease is worth it at the rate you're actually being offered

Sometimes the answer is "yes, even at this rate". Sometimes it's "no — a [cash purchase or conventional car loan](../start-here/is-it-worth-it/) wins at 15% effective". The [novated lease calculator](/calculator/) is the way to settle that for your own numbers.

## What employers should be doing

A short note for any HR or procurement reader who's landed here.

The signs of a salary packaging arrangement that genuinely serves employees:

- **Panel of 3+ providers**, with the panel refreshed every 2–3 years.
- **Explicit permission for BYO / self-managed** novated leasing.
- **Annual disclosure to staff** of any sign-on fees, rebates, or volume bonuses received from contracted providers. Some employers — including elements of the **Queensland government** — already require their salary packaging providers to disclose internal commissions, which is the right direction.
- **Transparent comparison tooling** rather than a single-provider portal that forecloses comparison.

Exclusive single-provider contracts may be administratively simpler. But they impose a structural cost on employees that the employer is rarely transparent about — and the cost is paid by the people the salary packaging benefit was nominally created to help.

---

## Key takeaway

If you remember nothing else:

> **An exclusive single-provider novated lease arrangement removes the competitive pressure that normally keeps rates honest. The cost is paid by the employee — quietly, over years — through inflated effective interest rates. The fair-margin range is $3–5k per 5-year lease; the predatory range is $10–20k. Spot the structure early, don't take the contracted provider's word for what's available, get external quotes anyway, and use the calculator to decide whether the lease still makes sense at the rate you're actually being offered.**
